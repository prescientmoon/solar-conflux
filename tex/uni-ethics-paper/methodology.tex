The question of whether companies should be allowed to vary pricing based on statistical profiling is a very broad one. Naturally, we will analyze the issue by looking at various subsets of the risk factors involved in profiling. Such subsets can be defined based on criteria such as whether the agent is responsible for the risk factor, whether the risk factor is causally linked to health costs, and whether the agent can be reasonably expected to know about the aforementioned causal link.

Before we proceed, we will outline a few examples of risk factors commonly used for statistical profiling that fall onto the different sides of each of our criteria. For the first criterion, age is a risk factor the agent is not responsible for, while their body mass index is one they usually are. For the second criterion, people who smoke are more likely to develop lung cancer~\cite{effects-of-smoking}, leading to high medical costs~\cite{nl-lung-cancer-costs}, hence the individual's smoking habits represent a risk factor that is causally responsible for the increased health costs. On the other hand, a person's place of living could be used for profiling if correlation was observed with health costs, all without requiring the causality link to exist. As for the last criteria, while people in developed countries can, in the 21st century, be reasonably expected to know about the health risks of smoking~\cite{smoking-effect-knowledge}, this would not have been the case a hundred years ago\footnote{According to~\cite{smoking-in-the-50s}, the turning point regarding research into the effects of smoking happened in the 1950s, thus people would have had no reasonable way to know about the causal link before then.}.

The agents at play in our analysis are the insurance companies, and the individuals who pay for the insurance.

\subsection{The ethical framework}
We will analyse the ramifications of the different risk factors through two frameworks: an ethical-based and a policy-based one. For our ethical analysis, we will consider the ethical theories of consequentialism and deontology. Our analysis is primarily based on~\cite{odihi20}.

In particular, we will use utilitarianism (a form of consequentialism). According to~\cite[Chapter~7]{elemofmp}, utilitarianism is based around the ``Principle of Utility'', that is, the principle that all actions should attempt to maximize the so called ``utility'' produced. Intuitively, our analysis through the lens of utilitarianism will focus on the consequences profiling using different classes of risk factors has on the parties involved.

Second, we will analyse the issues through the lens of deontology. Kant's ethics (i.e.~deontology) are based around the idea that humans should ``always be treated as an end and never as a means only'', which he formulate into a categorical imperative (a law of morality, which all actions must abide by): ``Act so that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in that of another, always as an end and never as a means only''~\cite[Chapter~10]{elemofmp}. A more concrete equivalent formulation of this imperative, as stated by~\cite[Section~4.2.1]{syllabus} is the following:~''Act only according to that maxim whereby you can, at the same time, will that it should become a universal law''.

Using Kant's theory to analyse statistical profiling turns out to be quite challenging, as making precise arguments that go beyond the fact that, in some vague sense, health insurance companies treat humans as mere data points when performing statistical profiling turns out to be no easy task. We will instead turn our attention to a specific form of deontology — \emph{contractualism}. In~\cite[Page~153]{what-we-owe}, Scanlon summarises the underlying principle as follows: ``An act is wrong if its performance under the circumstances would be disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced, general agreement''. We will thus analyse whether statistical profiling (or the lack thereof) violates any such principles.

Moreover, we will also make use of John Rawls's theory of contractualism, which is centered around the famous \emph{veil of ignorance}. According to~\cite[Section~2]{sep-contractualism}, ``Rawls places the parties to his agreement behind a veil of ignorance, where they do not know many key facts about their own identity. This is to ensure that the resulting principles of justice embody Rawls’s commitment to liberal neutrality''. Rawls's contractualism is an useful form of analysis, as it puts forth the inherent anti-symmetry introduced by statistical profiling and its consequences.
