Rawls uses his famous \emph{veil of ignorance} to argue that rational agents agree on principles of justice that ensure the best outcome for those that are currently in the worst position. Intuitively, if one cannot tell whether they will end up in poor or good health, then they would rationally only favour inequalities which improve everyone's situation. Statistical profiling fails this test, as it requires those in poor situations to pay more, thus sending them a downwards spiral of degrading circumstances, since wealth and health are strongly postively correlated~\cite{health-socdec}. Indeed, under Rawls's contractualism, one should then side against profiling, since they might end up on the poor end of the spectrum once the veil is lifted up.

Scanlon argued that moral principles are those that cannot reasonably be rejected: ``In order for a principle to be reasonably rejectable there must be some relevant standpoint from which people typically have good reason''~\cite[Page~218]{what-we-owe}. Of course, those in poor conditions can reasonably reject the principle of allowing health insurance companies to perform statistical profiling, by bringing up arguments like the fact it puts a larger burden on those who already find themselves in terrible situations. On the other hand, healthy people wouldn't be able to reasonably make the converse argument for supporting statistical profiling, since they do not find themselves in the same terrible situations.

Of course, our analysis so far has been quite rough. In reality, the arguments' effectiveness varies greatly based on the specific risk factor being used for profiling. For one, an unhealthy person would have a hard time reasonably rejecting statistical profiling if they themselves are responsible for their poor health condition, thus making profiling permissible under Scanlon's theory. One could for example similarly argue under Rawls's theory that a person who smokes could reasonably agree even when under the veil of ignorance that they deserve to pay more for their insurance, regardless of whether they are in fact sick once the veil is lifted up.

Can we then conclude that statistical profiling is permissible, perhaps even preferable, for risk factors the individual is responsible for? As~\cite[Section~3]{odihi20} discusses, the issue of responsibility is a lot more complicated than it might sound. We define \emph{responsibility} the same way as the aforementioned paper: ``an agent is responsible for X if X is a result, foreseeable by the agent, of a free choice of theirs''. We will first consider risk factors that fall under the intersection of the three criteria defined in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. Examples of risk factors which fall under this umbrella include one's body-mass index, smoking habits, and alcohol consumption.

The main complication when it comes to the three risk factors mentioned above comes from the fact they are usually bound to addictive behaviours. For one, those behaviours are influenced by both environmental factors~\cite{agrawal-gen-addict}, and genetic predispositions~\cite{schizo-addict}. This suggests practical difficulties in assessing what percentage of one's risk factors they bear the responsibility for, especially when multiple of the aforementioned factors compound.

It now remains to look at risk factors which fail to satisfy either of the last two of our three criteria (we are still discussing things in the context of responsibility, thus we treat the first criteria as always being required). If the risk factor at hand is not causally linked to the increase in health cost, then one could reasonably argue they might be responsible for the risk factor itself, but not for the health cost incurred downstream, thus siding against profiling in Scanlon's theory. If the causal link exists, but without the individual's knowledge (e.g.~in the case of smoking in the 1920s), then an affected individual can similarly argue they are not responsible for the incurred health costs, as part of our definition of responsibility was for the outcome to be \emph{foreseeable}, leading to the same conclusion.

While there's no one definitive answer we can draw here, we conclude that under the two considered versions of contractualism, statistical profiling is to some extent permissible only when the risk factor satisfies the three outlined criteria, i.e. when the risk factor is causally linked to the health costs in a way foreseeable by the individual, while falling under the individual's responsibilities.
